# Janke LLP Website - Project Instructions

This repository contains the new Janke LLP website.

Read this file before making material design, content, UX, architecture, or implementation decisions.

---

## 1. Source of truth

The finalized Janke LLP brand materials and Website Design & Build Context are the primary source of truth for this project.

Expected source material should be stored under a structure similar to:

```text
docs/
  brand/
    Janke_LLP_Website_Design_and_Build_Context_v1.docx
    finalized-brand-package/
      ...
```

If the brand package is still zipped, unpack it under `docs/brand/` before relying on it for implementation.

Before making material design or content decisions:

1. Read this `CLAUDE.md`.
2. Inspect all relevant files under `docs/brand/`.
3. Treat the finalized Brand Guide, Brand Quick Reference, Website Design & Build Context, and approved templates as authoritative.
4. If two source documents conflict, prefer the most recent finalized/approved document.
5. If a conflict cannot be resolved from the source material, ask for clarification rather than guessing.

Do not silently replace supplied brand direction with generic design conventions, competitor patterns, or personal preference.

Do not modify source brand documents unless explicitly asked.

---

## 2. Firm positioning

Janke LLP is an advisory-led CPA firm.

The website should communicate sophisticated advice with practical clarity for Saskatchewan businesses, families, professionals, agricultural clients, and organizations.

The brand should feel:

- sophisticated but approachable;
- confident, calm, and practical;
- advisory-led rather than commodity-accounting-led;
- premium without feeling luxurious, flashy, or pretentious;
- professional and plainspoken;
- distinctly Janke LLP rather than visually derivative of a national accounting firm;
- rooted in Saskatchewan without relying on rural cliches.

The firm should be presented as **firm-first, with visible experts**. Individual professionals should be credible and accessible without making the brand dependent on one personality.

---

## 3. Priority audiences

Design information architecture, landing-page paths, calls to action, and content priorities around these primary audiences:

1. Owner-managed and family businesses
2. Agriculture and agribusiness
3. Legal, financial, and medical professionals
4. Clients dealing with succession, tax, and family wealth planning

The website should primarily organize content around **client needs**, supported by industry-specific pages and proof points where appropriate.

## 4. Brand colour system

The website should remain overwhelmingly black and white.

Approved colours:

- Near-black: `#111111`
- White: `#FFFFFF`
- Prairie Gold: `#B58A2C`
- Bright Prairie Gold: `#D4A842`
- Warm White: `#F7F5F0`
- Stone: `#C9C5BD`

Target visual balance:

- approximately 90-95% black and white;
- no more than approximately 5-10% Prairie Gold;
- use gold as a restrained accent, not as a dominant field colour.

Use `#D4A842` where additional contrast is required on black.
Use `#B58A2C` for rules, markers, icons, small accents, and restrained emphasis.

Avoid introducing unapproved brand colours.

In particular, do not use teal, sage, navy, purple, or generic accounting-firm blue as brand colours.

Do not use gradients unless there is a compelling, brand-consistent reason and explicit approval.

---

## 5. Typography

Approved typography direction:

- Gilroy for marketing headings and display typography where licensed web font files are legitimately available in the repository.
- Arial for formal/operational body typography.
- Manrope is the approved web-oriented fallback where appropriate.

For the website:

1. Use Gilroy for headings if properly licensed webfont assets are available.
2. If Gilroy is unavailable for lawful web use, use the approved fallback rather than sourcing or embedding an unlicensed font.
3. Use a highly readable body system consistent with the supplied brand guide.
4. Maintain strong hierarchy, generous line height, and comfortable reading measure.
5. Do not shrink text merely to make layouts fit.

Do not distribute, expose, or publish font files outside the intended website build.

---

## 6. Logo use

Use the approved Janke LLP logo assets without structural modification.

Preferred logo treatments are monochrome.

Do not:

- recolour the logo arbitrarily;
- add gradients, shadows, outlines, or effects;
- distort proportions;
- attach the supporting line directly to the logo;
- recreate the logo from text when approved artwork exists.

The supporting line:

**Grounded advice. Grow better.**

may be used selectively in appropriate marketing contexts, but it is not part of the logo lockup and should not appear everywhere.

## 7. Voice and writing style

Website voice should be:

- professional;
- plainspoken;
- intelligent without sounding academic;
- warm and conversational where appropriate;
- confident without hype;
- useful and specific;
- restrained rather than sales-heavy.

Prefer short, direct sentences and concrete language.

Avoid:

- jargon for its own sake;
- vague claims such as "world-class" unless specifically supported;
- inflated marketing language;
- generic accounting cliches;
- copy that could belong to any CPA firm;
- unnecessary exclamation marks;
- invented facts or proof points.

Formal legal, tax, or technical content may use a more restrained tone where required.

## 8. Approved strategic messaging

Use these ideas where appropriate and supported by context.

Primary conversational prompt:

**What does better look like?**

Primary CTA:

**Start the conversation**

Geographic supporting proof point:

**Rooted in Northeast Saskatchewan. Serving clients throughout Saskatchewan.**

Geography is a supporting proof point, not the lead positioning message.

Calls to action should emphasize learning, growth, improvement, opportunity, better decisions, and helping clients realize what better looks like.

Pricing-related messaging, where relevant, should emphasize:

- clear expectations;
- transparent scope;
- fees proportionate to complexity and value.

Do not invent fee amounts, guarantees, savings, outcomes, or claims.

## 9. Website information architecture

The website system should be capable of supporting at minimum:

- Home
- Services / Client Needs
- Industries
- About Janke LLP
- Our People
- Insights / Resources
- Careers
- Contact

It should also support:

- individual service/client-need pages;
- industry pages;
- professional profile pages;
- insight/article pages;
- recruitment pages;
- client stories/case studies where approved;
- reusable landing pages and campaign content.

Navigation should be **client-needs-led**, with industry pages providing additional relevance and proof.

Do not overcomplicate the primary navigation.

---

## 10. Homepage direction

The homepage should clearly establish:

1. who Janke LLP is;
2. how the firm helps clients improve, grow, navigate complexity, and make better decisions;
3. clear paths based on client needs;
4. important advisory capabilities and services;
5. Saskatchewan credibility without making geography the entire story;
6. visible human expertise;
7. supported proof/trust elements;
8. useful insight/resource content where available;
9. a strong closing invitation using the approved CTA philosophy.

A strong closing section may use:

**What does better look like?**

with:

**Start the conversation**

Do not treat any concept mockup as a pixel-perfect mandate. Use mockups as directional input while following the full approved brand system.

---

## 11. Service and industry pages

Service/client-need and industry pages should be structured for decision-making rather than as encyclopedic lists.

Where source content supports it, help visitors understand:

- the situation or need;
- why it matters;
- how Janke LLP approaches it;
- relevant capabilities;
- who the work is suited to;
- what a productive next step looks like.

Use the approved source documents for final page structures. Do not invent services or capabilities that are not supported.

Cross-link related client needs, industries, professionals, insights, and calls to action where useful.

---

## 12. People and professional profiles

The website should make Janke LLP professionals visible and credible while keeping the firm brand primary.

Do not invent:

- designations;
- biographies;
- roles;
- years of experience;
- community involvement;
- specialties;
- credentials;
- awards;
- affiliations.

Use supplied staff information only.

If staff biography or headshot standards have not yet been finalized, build the component system to accommodate future approved content without inventing the missing standards.

---

## 13. Careers and recruitment

Recruitment content should reflect these three approved pillars:

1. Sophisticated work experience using best-in-class tools and systems
2. Visible impact on the lives of clients and the community
3. A very high-quality work-life environment

Keep recruitment content credible, specific, and human.

Do not make unsupported promises about compensation, flexibility, benefits, progression, or workplace policies.

## 14. Photography and imagery

Preferred photography:

- real Janke LLP people;
- real clients when properly authorized;
- real Saskatchewan environments;
- polished but genuine;
- natural interaction;
- strong composition;
- restrained editing;
- minimal generic stock photography.

Avoid:

- handshake stock photos;
- staged boardroom cliches;
- generic skyscraper imagery;
- cliche wheat-field imagery used as a substitute for substance;
- artificial "corporate diversity" stock scenes;
- overly glossy or cinematic treatment inconsistent with the firm.

Photography should reinforce credibility, humanity, place, and confidence.

If final photography is unavailable, create structurally correct image placeholders with descriptive labels for the content team rather than inserting misleading stock imagery.

## 15. Testimonials and client stories

Client stories and testimonials require project-specific consent.

Testimonials may be lightly edited for clarity only when the client receives and approves the final version.

Do not fabricate:

- testimonial quotes;
- client identities;
- results;
- metrics;
- tax savings;
- transaction values;
- outcomes.

Where final approved content is unavailable, use clearly marked placeholders or TODOs.

## 16. UI design principles

The digital experience should rely on:

- strong typography;
- generous whitespace;
- excellent alignment;
- editorial composition;
- simple grids;
- restrained Prairie Gold rules and accents;
- large, purposeful imagery;
- clear hierarchy;
- calm, deliberate pacing;
- subtle and purposeful interaction.

Avoid:

- excessive card grids;
- rounded SaaS-style component systems;
- pill-shaped UI everywhere;
- glassmorphism;
- gratuitous shadows;
- decorative gradients;
- glowing effects;
- noisy background textures;
- unnecessary carousels;
- excessive animation;
- visually busy dashboards;
- spreadsheet-like marketing layouts;
- template-library aesthetics.

Use rounded corners only where functionally or visually justified. They should not define the brand.

---

## 17. Motion

Motion should be understated and purposeful.

Good uses include:

- subtle entrance transitions;
- navigation feedback;
- restrained image or rule movement;
- state changes that improve comprehension.

Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.

Do not add an animation library solely for decorative effects.

Avoid scroll-jacking, excessive parallax, autoplay distractions, or effects that compete with content.

---

## 18. Accessibility

Target WCAG 2.2 AA where practical.

At minimum:

- use semantic HTML;
- maintain logical heading hierarchy;
- ensure full keyboard navigation;
- provide visible focus states;
- meet colour contrast requirements;
- provide meaningful alt-text handling;
- label form controls properly;
- expose validation errors accessibly;
- support zoom and text resizing;
- avoid conveying meaning by colour alone;
- respect reduced-motion preferences;
- make interactive target sizes practical;
- test mobile and desktop navigation with keyboard and assistive-technology considerations.

Accessibility is a design requirement, not a final QA add-on.

---

## 19. Responsive design

Design mobile-first and verify at multiple viewport widths.

The site should feel intentional on:

- small mobile;
- large mobile;
- tablet;
- laptop;
- standard desktop;
- large desktop.

Do not simply stack desktop components vertically on mobile.

Pay particular attention to:

- navigation;
- heading scale;
- content measure;
- image crops;
- comparison layouts;
- tables;
- forms;
- CTA placement;
- long professional names and titles.

---

## 20. Performance

Prioritize fast loading and minimal client-side JavaScript.

Use:

- optimized responsive images;
- modern image formats where supported;
- lazy loading below the fold;
- sensible font loading;
- code splitting where appropriate;
- server-rendered/static content where appropriate;
- minimal third-party scripts;
- efficient component architecture.

Avoid dependencies that duplicate native browser or framework functionality.

Do not add heavy UI or animation libraries without a clear need.

---

## 21. SEO and discoverability

Build sound technical SEO foundations:

- semantic page structure;
- descriptive unique page titles;
- useful meta descriptions;
- canonical URL handling;
- clean human-readable URLs;
- crawlable navigation;
- appropriate Open Graph/social metadata;
- structured data only where accurate and appropriate;
- XML sitemap support;
- robots handling;
- useful internal linking.

Do not keyword-stuff.

Content should be written for people first.

Do not invent locations, services, staff, reviews, awards, or credentials for SEO purposes.

---

## 22. Forms and conversion

Forms should be simple, trustworthy, and accessible.

Ask only for information needed at that stage.

For primary contact conversion, favor the approved conversational framing:

**What does better look like?**

and:

**Start the conversation**

Do not imply that submitting a form establishes a professional engagement or creates a client relationship.

Leave appropriate room for legal/privacy language where required.

Never commit secrets, API keys, CRM credentials, email credentials, or private submission data to the repository.

## 24. Engineering approach

Before changing code:

1. inspect the repository;
2. understand the existing stack;
3. identify build, lint, test, and type-check commands;
4. identify routing, styling, content, asset, and component conventions;
5. preserve working architecture unless there is a strong reason to change it.

Do not replace an established stack merely because you prefer another technology.

If the repository has **no existing application stack**, selecting the stack is a material architecture decision. Present a concise recommendation and obtain approval before scaffolding the production application unless the user has already specified the stack.

Prefer:

- TypeScript where supported;
- semantic components;
- small composable project-specific components;
- minimal dependencies;
- understandable folder structure;
- maintainable content modelling;
- predictable naming;
- straightforward CSS rather than unnecessary abstraction.

Do not build a large generic design-system library before the site demonstrates a real need for it.

---

## 25. Design tokens

Implement centralized design tokens rather than scattering literal values throughout components.

Tokens should cover at minimum:

- brand colours;
- typography;
- font sizes;
- line heights;
- spacing;
- content widths;
- layout gutters;
- borders/rules;
- breakpoints;
- motion timing where needed.

Use semantic token names where practical, for example:

```text
--color-bg
--color-text
--color-accent
--color-surface-warm
--color-border
--space-section
--content-reading
--content-wide
```

Do not create numerous nearly identical shades unless the design genuinely requires them.

---

## 26. Component philosophy

Prefer a small set of excellent reusable primitives rather than a large generic component library.

Likely reusable patterns include:

- Header
- Mobile navigation
- Footer
- Container
- Section
- Eyebrow
- Heading group
- Primary CTA
- Secondary text link
- Editorial split layout
- Service/client-need link
- Industry link
- Professional preview
- Insight/article preview
- Quote/testimonial treatment
- Image treatment
- Contact CTA
- Form primitives

Do not force every section into the same card component.

Allow editorial variation while keeping typography, spacing, alignment, and interaction consistent.

---

## 27. Content modelling

Structure content so it can later move to a CMS without requiring a complete frontend rewrite.

Separate content from presentation where practical.

Model recurring content types consistently, such as:

- services/client needs;
- industries;
- people;
- insights;
- client stories;
- careers/jobs.

Do not introduce a CMS simply because one might be useful later. If CMS selection is required, treat it as a material architecture decision and request approval.

---

## 28. Repository hygiene

Maintain a clean repository.

Do not commit:

- secrets;
- `.env` values;
- generated build output that should be ignored;
- large unnecessary source binaries;
- duplicate brand assets;
- temporary screenshots;
- local editor files;
- dependency caches.

Use clear filenames and folders.

Keep source brand materials distinguishable from production website assets.

Preserve a clear boundary between:

- source/reference documents;
- website content;
- website code;
- production assets.

---

## 29. Approval workflow

Work autonomously on ordinary implementation choices.

Ask for approval before:

- selecting a production stack when none exists;
- materially changing an established architecture;
- changing the approved brand system;
- adding a new brand colour;
- replacing typography;
- materially changing navigation architecture;
- selecting a CMS;
- adding substantial third-party services;
- adding analytics/marketing technology that affects privacy;
- introducing unsupported factual content;
- propagating an unapproved visual direction across the whole site.

For initial visual development, establish one strong vertical slice first - normally the homepage - before propagating the system to all pages.

The goal is to correct major design decisions once, not repeatedly across many pages.

---

## 30. Quality assurance

Before reporting a material implementation as complete, run the relevant available checks.

At minimum, where supported:

- install/dependency validation;
- lint;
- type check;
- automated tests;
- production build;
- responsive review;
- keyboard review;
- basic accessibility review;
- broken-link review;
- browser console error review.

Fix problems rather than merely listing them when the fix is reasonably within scope.

If a check cannot be performed, say so explicitly.

---

## 31. Visual QA

For every major page, review the rendered result rather than relying only on code.

Check:

- hierarchy;
- whitespace;
- alignment;
- reading width;
- image crops;
- heading wraps;
- mobile layouts;
- focus states;
- hover states;
- long content;
- empty states;
- form errors;
- footer behaviour;
- consistency with the approved brand.

Avoid propagating a component that has not been visually reviewed in context.

---

## 32. Browser and device expectations

Use standards-based implementation.

At minimum, target current mainstream versions of:

- Chrome;
- Edge;
- Safari;
- Firefox.

Ensure the experience remains usable when JavaScript is delayed or partially unavailable where the architecture allows.

Do not optimize solely for a single desktop browser.

---

## 33. External services

Do not add or configure third-party services without clear need.

Potential later integrations may include:

- analytics;
- CRM/contact routing;
- email;
- maps;
- CMS;
- scheduling;
- recruitment systems;
- Figma or other design tooling.

Treat services involving personal information, tracking, cookies, or credentials as requiring explicit approval and appropriate privacy consideration.

---

## 34. AI-assisted content

AI may assist with drafting and transformation, but generated copy is not automatically approved website content.

When producing substantive Janke LLP copy:

- ground it in approved source material;
- clearly identify unsupported placeholders;
- avoid fabricated expertise or proof;
- preserve professional review for technical, tax, legal, financial, and client-specific statements.

Do not publish generated testimonials, client stories, or professional credentials.

---

## 35. Project status reporting

At the end of a material work session, provide a concise report including:

1. what was inspected;
2. what was built or changed;
3. important design or architecture decisions;
4. files/components materially changed;
5. unsupported content represented by placeholders/TODOs;
6. lint/type/test/build results;
7. accessibility or responsive issues still open;
8. decisions requiring approval;
9. recommended next step.

Do not hide uncertainty.

5. Build one complete homepage vertical slice

7. Build Services / Client Needs
8. Build Industries
9. Build About Janke LLP
10. Build Our People
11. Build Insights / Resources
12. Build Careers
13. Build Contact

## 38. Final principle

The Janke LLP website should make sophisticated professional advice feel clear, grounded, human, and useful.

When choosing between:

- more decoration and more clarity;
- more features and more focus;
- more jargon and plainer language;
- more colour and stronger black-and-white composition;
- more components and better editorial hierarchy;

prefer clarity, focus, plain language, black-and-white discipline, and editorial quality.

The website should feel like the digital expression of Janke LLP's advice:

**simple, straightforward, thoughtful, and valuable.**
